Irregular Periods in Perimenopause

Irregular periods means your cycle — its length, its predictability, how heavy or light it is — has changed from what's normal for you. Cycles might come closer together, further apart, skip entirely, or vary more from month to month than they used to. For many people, this kind of cycle change is the earliest noticeable sign of perimenopause, sometimes showing up before hot flushes or mood changes do.

Where this fits on the Greene Scale

Honestly: it doesn't. The Greene Climacteric Scale measures the severity of psychological, physical, vasomotor, and sexual symptoms — it has no item for menstrual cycle changes, because cycle tracking (dates, flow, and pattern over time) is a different kind of data than a symptom-severity rating. If you're also noticing psychological, physical, or vasomotor symptoms alongside your cycle changes, though, those are worth capturing — the free Greene Climacteric Scale self-assessment covers that part of the picture in about five minutes.

What's actually known

As ovarian function becomes less predictable during perimenopause, ovulation happens less consistently, which is what drives cycle length and flow to change — often shorter and closer together earlier in the transition, then longer and more spaced out as it progresses, though this pattern varies a lot between individuals. Because this variability is expected, current NICE guidance recommends diagnosing perimenopause and menopause based on symptoms and age rather than a hormone blood test in most people over 45, since hormone levels fluctuate significantly during this transition and a single test result can be misleading.

What tends to help

There isn't a lifestyle intervention that "fixes" cycle irregularity itself — it's a signal of a hormonal process, not a symptom to manage the way you might manage a hot flush. What actually helps is tracking: knowing your own pattern makes it much easier to tell an unremarkable perimenopausal shift apart from something that needs attention, and gives a doctor something concrete to work from if you do need to discuss it.

When to escalate to a clinician

See a doctor for bleeding that's very heavy (soaking through protection every hour or two), periods closer together than every three weeks, bleeding between periods or after sex, or any bleeding after twelve full months without a period. None of these should be assumed to be ordinary perimenopausal change without a doctor ruling out other causes first.

Frequently asked questions

Are irregular periods usually the first sign of perimenopause?

For many people, yes — cycle changes are often the earliest noticeable sign, sometimes appearing before hot flushes, night sweats, or mood changes. That's not universal, and this page describes a common pattern rather than a rule that applies to everyone.

Does the Greene Climacteric Scale measure irregular periods?

No. The Greene Climacteric Scale measures the severity of psychological, physical, vasomotor, and sexual symptoms — it doesn't include a menstrual cycle item. Cycle tracking is a different kind of data (dates and bleeding patterns) rather than a symptom-severity rating, which is why it isn't part of this particular scale.

Do I need a blood test to confirm perimenopause because of irregular periods?

Usually not. Current NICE guidance recommends diagnosing perimenopause and menopause based on symptoms and age rather than a hormone blood test in most people over 45, since hormone levels fluctuate significantly during this transition and a single test result can be misleading.

What kind of irregular bleeding needs a doctor's attention?

Bleeding that's very heavy (soaking through protection every hour or two), periods closer together than every three weeks, bleeding between periods or after sex, or any bleeding after twelve full months without a period, all warrant a doctor's evaluation rather than being assumed to be ordinary perimenopausal change.
