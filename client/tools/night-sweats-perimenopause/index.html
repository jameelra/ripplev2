Night Sweats in Perimenopause

Night sweats are hot flushes that happen while you're asleep — a sudden rise in heat and sweating intense enough to wake you, sometimes drenching enough that you need to change clothes or bedding. They're one of the symptoms most classically associated with perimenopause, and because they interrupt sleep directly, they often make other symptoms — fatigue, brain fog, irritability — feel worse than they would on their own.

Where this fits on the Greene Scale

This is one of the symptoms tracked in the Greene Climacteric Scale — item 20, "Sweating at night," one of two items making up the scale's Vasomotor subscale, alongside item 19, "Hot flushes." What's actually known

Night sweats share the same underlying mechanism as daytime hot flushes: as estrogen fluctuates during perimenopause, the hypothalamus — the brain's temperature-regulation center — becomes more sensitive to small changes in core body temperature, triggering an exaggerated heat-dissipation response. At night, this is compounded by the fact that sleep itself already involves changes in thermoregulation, which is part of why nocturnal episodes can feel more disruptive than daytime ones. Research using objective sleep measurements has found that people with frequent vasomotor symptoms show measurably lower sleep efficiency and less time in slow-wave (deep) sleep than those without them (Kravitz et al., 2008).

What tends to help

Bedroom cooling — a cooler room temperature, moisture-wicking bedding, layered sleepwear — is a straightforward, non-hormonal first step many people find helpful. Alcohol is a vasodilator, meaning it widens blood vessels near the skin, and reducing evening alcohol intake is a commonly reported way to lessen the frequency or intensity of episodes. Hormone therapy is the treatment most studied for vasomotor symptoms generally, with the current position statement from The Menopause Society (published under its former name, NAMS, in 2022) addressing its role for symptomatic people within the appropriate age and treatment window — but whether it's right for you is a decision to make with a doctor based on your history, not something this page can recommend either way.

When to escalate to a clinician

See a doctor if night sweats are occurring most nights, disrupting sleep enough to affect your daytime functioning, or accompanied by unexplained weight loss, fever, or symptoms outside the usual perimenopausal pattern — those combinations are worth ruling out other causes for. It's reasonable to bring a simple log of frequency and severity to that conversation rather than trying to describe it from memory.

Frequently asked questions

What causes night sweats in perimenopause?

Night sweats share the same underlying mechanism as daytime hot flushes: as estrogen fluctuates, the brain's temperature-regulation center becomes more sensitive to small changes in body temperature, triggering sudden heat and sweating. At night, this also disrupts sleep architecture, which is why night sweats are strongly linked to poor sleep quality.

When do perimenopausal night sweats stop?

There's no single, reliably sourced timeline that applies to everyone, and this page won't invent one — duration varies significantly from person to person. What's well established is that night sweats are a vasomotor symptom tied to hormonal fluctuation during the menopause transition, and tracking your own pattern over time is more useful than a general average.

Are night sweats one item on the Greene Climacteric Scale?

Yes. "Sweating at night" is item 20, one of two items in the Greene Climacteric Scale's Vasomotor subscale, alongside item 19, "Hot flushes."

Does alcohol make night sweats worse?

Alcohol is a vasodilator, meaning it widens blood vessels near the skin, which is part of the same mechanism that produces a hot flush or night sweat. Many people notice fewer or less severe episodes after reducing evening alcohol intake, though individual response varies.
