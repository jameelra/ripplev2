Anxiety and Mood Changes in Perimenopause

Many people notice new or intensified anxiety, irritability, low mood, or tearfulness during perimenopause — sometimes for the first time in their lives, sometimes as a worsening of something they've managed for years. This page describes that pattern; it isn't diagnosing your own experience, and it isn't a substitute for an assessment by a doctor if what you're feeling concerns you.

Where this fits on the Greene Scale

These are symptoms tracked in the Greene Climacteric Scale's 11-item Psychological subscale, which splits into two groups: an Anxiety group (a racing heart, feeling tense or on edge, trouble sleeping, feeling excitable, panic, and difficulty concentrating) and a Depressed Mood group (fatigue, loss of interest, feeling unhappy, crying spells, and irritability). What's actually known

Estrogen and progesterone both influence the brain systems most involved in regulating anxiety and mood. As these hormones fluctuate during perimenopause, that regulation can become less stable — which is the leading explanation for why some people experience anxiety for the first time, or find existing anxiety harder to manage, during this transition. Because perimenopausal anxiety can resemble a primary anxiety disorder, it's sometimes treated the same way by default; NICE guideline NG23 notes there is no clear evidence that antidepressants ease low mood or anxiety caused by menopause itself in people who haven't been diagnosed with depression, and that hormone therapy should be considered for depressive symptoms that don't meet the criteria for a depression diagnosis (NG23, recommendation 1.5.21).

What tends to help

This is useful context for a conversation, not a reason to second-guess a prescription you've already been given — antidepressants remain an appropriate, evidence-based option once depression or an anxiety disorder is actually diagnosed, or when hormone therapy isn't suitable or preferred. Regular physical activity and attention to sleep are general lifestyle measures reflected in NICE's broader guidance on perimenopause management. Whether hormone therapy is appropriate for you specifically is a decision to make with a doctor based on your history — this page can't and won't recommend it either way.

When to escalate to a clinician

See a doctor if anxiety is significantly affecting your daily functioning, if you're experiencing panic attacks, or if you're having thoughts of self-harm — the last of these warrants urgent attention, regardless of what's causing it. It's also worth raising if what you're feeling doesn't seem to fit the usual pattern you'd expect from your other perimenopausal symptoms, since that's a useful signal for a doctor to investigate further rather than assume.

Frequently asked questions

Can perimenopause cause anxiety even without a prior history of it?

Yes, new-onset anxiety during perimenopause is commonly reported. Estrogen and progesterone both influence neurotransmitter systems involved in anxiety regulation, so hormonal fluctuation is a plausible contributor even for people with no previous anxiety history. This page describes a pattern — it isn't a diagnosis of your own experience.

Will antidepressants fix perimenopausal anxiety?

It depends on the cause. NICE guideline NG23 notes there is no clear evidence that antidepressants ease low mood or anxiety caused by menopause itself in people who haven't been diagnosed with depression, and that hormone therapy should be considered for depressive symptoms that don't meet the criteria for a depression diagnosis. Antidepressants remain an appropriate, evidence-based choice once depression or an anxiety disorder is actually diagnosed, or when hormone therapy isn't suitable or preferred — this is a conversation to have with a doctor, not a reason to distrust one.

Which Greene Climacteric Scale items relate to anxiety and mood?

The scale's 11-item Psychological subscale splits into two groups: an Anxiety group (heart racing, feeling tense, sleep difficulty, feeling excitable, panic attacks, and difficulty concentrating) and a Depressed Mood group (fatigue, loss of interest, feeling unhappy, crying spells, and irritability).

Is perimenopausal anxiety the same as a generalized anxiety disorder?

Not necessarily. This page doesn't diagnose either condition. What's known is that hormonal fluctuation can produce or worsen anxiety symptoms during perimenopause, and that distinguishing a hormonally-driven pattern from a primary anxiety disorder is something a doctor is best placed to assess.
