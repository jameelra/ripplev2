Vaginal Dryness in Perimenopause

Vaginal dryness is a reduction in the vagina's natural lubrication and tissue elasticity, which can cause irritation, discomfort, and pain during sex. It's one of the most under-discussed perimenopausal symptoms — not because it's rare, but because it's uncomfortable to bring up, and many clinicians don't ask about it directly. It's also treatable, and it tends to get worse rather than better if left unaddressed.

Where this fits on the Greene Scale

This one is worth explaining carefully. The Greene Climacteric Scale's item 21, "Loss of interest in sex," is reported on its own rather than added to the total score — but it's a question about interest (libido), not specifically about dryness or pain. The two are related — dryness and discomfort during sex are a common reason interest declines — but they aren't the same thing, which is exactly why Greene kept this item separate: a single question can't carry the same statistical weight as a full subscale, and folding a distinct construct like libido into another subscale's total would blur what's being measured. What's actually known

Vaginal tissue is rich in estrogen receptors, and estrogen maintains its thickness, elasticity, and natural lubrication. As estrogen declines during perimenopause and into menopause, vaginal tissue can thin and lubrication can decrease — sometimes grouped with related urinary symptoms under the term genitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM). Unlike vasomotor symptoms, which tend to ease over time for many people, genitourinary symptoms are understood to progress without treatment rather than resolve on their own (Portman & Gass, 2014).

What tends to help

This page doesn't make individual treatment recommendations — what's right for you depends on your personal and family history, and that's a conversation for a doctor. What's publicly documented is that over-the-counter vaginal moisturizers and lubricants can ease day-to-day discomfort without addressing the underlying tissue changes, and that localized (topical) vaginal estrogen has a distinct safety and absorption profile from systemic hormone therapy — a useful starting point if you want to raise treatment options with a doctor. The current position statement from The Menopause Society (published under its former name, NAMS, in 2022) addresses hormone therapy more broadly; ask your doctor how it applies to genitourinary symptoms specifically.

When to escalate to a clinician

See a doctor if you're experiencing pain during sex, recurrent urinary tract infections, urinary incontinence, or ongoing discomfort — these symptoms are highly treatable and shouldn't be accepted as an inevitable part of getting older. If it feels difficult to bring up, it's worth remembering that this is one of the more common and most treatable perimenopausal symptoms, not an unusual complaint.

Frequently asked questions

Why does perimenopause cause vaginal dryness?

Vaginal tissue is rich in estrogen receptors, and estrogen maintains its thickness, elasticity, and natural lubrication. As estrogen declines during perimenopause and into menopause, vaginal tissue can thin and lubrication can decrease — a pattern sometimes called genitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM).

Does vaginal dryness go away on its own like hot flushes do?

Not typically. Unlike vasomotor symptoms, which tend to ease over time for many people, genitourinary symptoms are understood to progress without treatment rather than resolve on their own — which is part of why it's worth raising with a doctor rather than waiting it out.

Why is sexual function scored separately on the Greene Climacteric Scale?

Item 21, "Loss of interest in sex," is reported on its own rather than added to the scale's total score. It's a single question about interest (libido), which is a different construct from vaginal dryness or discomfort — a single item also can't carry the same statistical weight as a multi-item subscale, which is why Greene kept it separate rather than folding it into a subscale total.
