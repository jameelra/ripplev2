Joint Pain in Perimenopause

Joint pain in perimenopause usually shows up as aching or stiffness in the hands, knees, hips, or shoulders — often on both sides of the body, and often worse first thing in the morning. It's a physical symptom that gets far less attention than hot flushes or mood changes, which means it's frequently attributed to "just getting older" rather than connected to the hormonal transition actually driving it.

Where this fits on the Greene Scale

This is one of the symptoms tracked in the Greene Climacteric Scale — item 16, "Muscle and joint pains," part of the Somatic subscale, which covers seven physical symptoms sometimes linked to hormonal change. What's actually known

Estrogen receptors are present throughout the musculoskeletal system — in cartilage, synovial membranes, and joint tissue — and estrogen has a documented anti-inflammatory effect there (Braidman et al., 2001). As estrogen declines and fluctuates during perimenopause, that protective effect is reduced, which is the leading explanation for new or worsening joint aches during this transition. There's also a specific, well-documented association between perimenopause and frozen shoulder (adhesive capsulitis), which has a peak incidence in midlife women — researchers have implicated hormonal factors in this pattern, though the exact mechanism isn't fully settled (Bunker, 2009).

What tends to help

Regular movement — particularly resistance and weight-bearing exercise — is a general, non-hormonal measure reflected in NICE's broader guidance on perimenopause and menopause management (NICE NG23), and it's commonly reported to reduce joint stiffness over time by strengthening the muscles that support affected joints. Some people find short-term relief from over-the-counter anti-inflammatory medication for flares, though that's not intended for ongoing daily use — worth checking with a pharmacist or doctor either way. Whether hormone therapy is appropriate for you specifically is a decision for a doctor based on your history, not something this page can recommend.

When to escalate to a clinician

See a doctor if a joint is visibly swollen, if morning stiffness lasts longer than 30 minutes, if you have a family history of rheumatoid arthritis, or if the pain is rapidly worsening rather than fluctuating. Blood tests can help a doctor distinguish perimenopausal joint pain from inflammatory arthritis — that distinction matters for treatment, and it's not one you can make from a symptom description alone.

Frequently asked questions

Why does perimenopause cause joint pain?

Estrogen receptors are present throughout the musculoskeletal system, and estrogen has an anti-inflammatory effect on joint tissue. As estrogen declines and fluctuates during perimenopause, that protective effect is reduced, which is the leading explanation for new or worsening joint aches and stiffness during this transition.

Is perimenopausal joint pain the same as arthritis?

Not necessarily, and this page doesn't diagnose either condition. Perimenopausal joint pain often presents as morning stiffness lasting under 30 minutes without visible joint swelling, which differs from the pattern typically seen in inflammatory arthritis — but a doctor, not a symptom description, is what can actually tell the two apart.

Is frozen shoulder linked to perimenopause?

There's a documented association: frozen shoulder (adhesive capsulitis) has a peak incidence in midlife women, which has led researchers to implicate hormonal factors in its development, though the exact mechanism isn't fully settled.

Does the Greene Climacteric Scale include joint pain?

Yes. "Muscle and joint pains" is item 16, part of the Greene Climacteric Scale's Somatic subscale, which covers seven physical symptoms sometimes linked to hormonal change.
