When Perimenopause Symptoms Get Dismissed as "Just Stress" or "Just Aging"

A lot of people describe some version of the same experience: bringing a real, disruptive symptom to a doctor, and leaving with "that's just stress," "that's normal at your age," or a prescription that treats the symptom's surface without addressing what might actually be driving it. This page doesn't diagnose your specific appointment — only you and your doctor have that context. What it can do is describe why this pattern happens as often as it's reported to, and it's mostly a systems problem, not an individual one.

What's actually behind this pattern

Three structural factors show up again and again in how this happens. First, appointment slots are short — often 10 to 15 minutes — which doesn't leave much room to unpack a broad, seemingly unrelated list of symptoms like brain fog, joint pain, and irregular periods all at once. Second, perimenopause has historically had thin coverage in medical training relative to how common it is, which means not every clinician has had deep, structured exposure to its full symptom range. Third, many perimenopausal symptoms genuinely overlap with other conditions — anxiety, depression, thyroid dysfunction, chronic fatigue — which can look similar from a brief description and reasonably prompt a clinician to consider those first. None of this excuses a dismissive appointment if you've had one. It does mean the more useful target for change is usually the system around the visit, not the person in the room.

Why guidance like NICE NG23 exists

One piece of evidence that this was a recognized, structural problem: NICE's guideline on menopause identification and management (NG23) specifically directs clinicians to diagnose perimenopause and menopause based on symptoms and age rather than defaulting to a hormone blood test in most people over 45. Guidance like that doesn't get written in a vacuum — it reflects an acknowledged need to standardize how perimenopause gets recognized in the first place, which is consistent with dismissal or under-recognition being common enough to need addressing at a system level.

What tends to actually help

Two things make the biggest practical difference, and both are about self-advocacy, not confrontation. Coming prepared with a clear, organized account of your symptoms — rather than describing them from memory under time pressure — makes it easier for a clinician to take in the full picture in a short visit. And if a pattern of dismissal does develop across multiple visits, having a factual, dated record of what was said gives you something concrete to bring to the next appointment, or to a different clinician, instead of relying on memory alone.

Frequently asked questions

Why do doctors sometimes attribute perimenopause symptoms to stress or aging?

Mostly for structural reasons rather than individual carelessness: short appointment slots that don't leave room to unpack a broad symptom list, historically thin coverage of perimenopause in medical training, and genuine symptom overlap with anxiety, depression, thyroid disorders, and chronic fatigue, all of which can look similar from a brief description.

Does this mean my doctor doesn't care about me?

Not necessarily, and this page can't tell you whether your specific experience was dismissal or a reasonable clinical judgment call — it describes a documented pattern, not your situation. Most of what gets described as dismissal traces back to systemic constraints, not individual malice.

What can I do if I feel like my symptoms aren't being taken seriously?

Two things tend to help: coming prepared with a clear, organized account of your symptoms, and keeping a factual record if a pattern of dismissal develops over multiple visits. Both are about advocating for yourself more effectively, not about catching a clinician being wrong.
