Perimenopause and Being Taken Seriously: What Changed, What Hasn't

A lot of the caution and confusion around perimenopause care traces back to one study, one headline, and two decades of guidance slowly catching up to a more complete picture. Understanding that history doesn't fix a bad appointment, but it does explain why the conversation about perimenopause care looks the way it does today — and why "the evidence changed" and "day-to-day care changed" are two different, and not fully synchronized, things.

2002: a single study reshapes a generation of care

In 2002, the Women's Health Initiative — a large randomized trial — published results suggesting combined hormone therapy increased the risk of breast cancer, stroke, and heart disease in the postmenopausal women studied (Rossouw et al., 2002). The headline coverage was immediate and severe: hormone therapy use dropped sharply almost overnight, among both patients and prescribers. For an entire generation of clinicians trained during or after this period, caution around hormone therapy became the reflexive default.

2013: the reanalysis that changed the picture

The original trial enrolled women across a wide age range, and many participants started treatment years after menopause began — a detail that got lost in the initial headlines. A later, more detailed analysis of the same trial data, broken down by age and time since menopause, found a meaningfully different picture for women who started treatment earlier in the transition (Manson et al., 2013). That distinction — age and timing at treatment initiation — turned out to matter as much as whether hormone therapy was used at all.

What current guidance actually says

That more nuanced picture is now reflected in current clinical guidance. The 2022 position statement from The Menopause Society (published under its former name, NAMS) states that for people under 60 or within 10 years of their final period, the benefit-risk balance for treating symptoms is generally favorable. Separately, NICE guideline NG23 supports individualized, symptom-based decision-making — including diagnosing perimenopause and menopause based on symptoms and age rather than defaulting to a hormone blood test in most people over 45. Neither guideline endorses hormone therapy for everyone, and neither rules it out by default; both point toward an individualized conversation.

Why the evidence changing hasn't fully changed care

If the science moved on, why do so many people still describe feeling dismissed? Mostly because a position statement changing doesn't automatically change what happens in a ten-minute appointment everywhere at once. Caution left over from the 2002 headlines is still part of how many clinicians were trained. Perimenopause has historically had thin coverage in medical curricula relative to how common it is. And appointment structures haven't been redesigned around the reality that perimenopause symptoms are numerous and often need more than a few minutes to unpack. None of that is a reason to distrust an individual doctor — it's a reason the gap between "what the evidence says" and "what a given appointment looks like" can persist even after the science has settled.

Where that leaves you

Knowing this history is useful preparation, not ammunition. It means you can walk into a conversation about hormone therapy, or any other treatment, understanding that current guidance is more nuanced than the 2002 headlines that still shape a lot of public perception — and that asking about your own individual risk-benefit picture, based on your age and time since your last period, is a reasonable, evidence-grounded question to bring.

Frequently asked questions

What did the 2002 Women's Health Initiative study actually find?

The WHI's 2002 report on combined hormone therapy found an increased risk of breast cancer, stroke, and heart disease in the postmenopausal women studied (Rossouw et al., 2002). Use of hormone therapy dropped sharply afterward, both among patients and prescribers.

Did later research change that picture?

Yes, in an important way. The original trial enrolled women across a wide age range, including many who started treatment years after menopause began. A later, more detailed analysis by age and time since menopause found a different risk-benefit picture for women who started treatment earlier in the transition (Manson et al., 2013).

What does current guidance actually say about hormone therapy?

The 2022 position statement from The Menopause Society (published under its former name, NAMS) states that for people under 60 or within 10 years of their final period, the benefit-risk balance for treating symptoms is generally favorable. NICE guideline NG23 similarly supports individualized, symptom-based decision-making rather than a blanket avoidance of hormone therapy.

If the guidance changed, why do people still describe being dismissed?

Guidance changing at the level of a position statement doesn't automatically change day-to-day practice everywhere at once. Short appointment slots, historically thin perimenopause coverage in medical training, and lingering caution left over from the 2002 headlines all still shape individual appointments, even where the underlying evidence has moved on.

Is Ripple recommending hormone therapy?

No. This page explains what the evidence and current guidance say, not what you personally should do. Whether hormone therapy is appropriate for you depends on your individual history, and that decision belongs to you and a doctor.
